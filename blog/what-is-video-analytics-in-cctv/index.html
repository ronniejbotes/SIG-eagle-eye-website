What is video analytics in CCTV, and who decides what a flag means?

By SIG Eagle Eye25 November 2026

Video analytics is software that reads what your cameras record and points out the moments that match what it was set up to look for. In plain words: something happened, in this place, at this time.

The software reads the footage and raises a flag when it sees a match. For an employer, the interesting part is what happens after that flag.

What the software does with your footage

There's a wide range. Motion detection, where the software notices movement against a fixed background, is among the simpler forms. More advanced systems track people or objects across a scene, detect a type of object ("for example a person or car"), and can go on to behaviour analysis.

Behaviour analysis is where it gets useful at work. Instead of "something moved at the loading bay", you get "someone entered the racking zone while a forklift was operating". That's a moment a safety officer would want to see.

There's a practical catch: software can only read what the camera shows it. A camera pointed into the sun, or a lens nobody has cleaned since installation, gives the software less to work with.

Why a recorder full of footage still leaves you without a case

Ordinary CCTV records everything. That's its job, and it does it well. The trouble starts when something goes wrong on the floor and somebody has to find the moment, work out which rule it relates to, and write it up in a way that makes sense to the person who has to decide what happens next.

Video analytics takes the finding off your hands. It can't do the rest.

A flag is a reason to look closer

Software sees pixels. It can see a person standing in a restricted zone. It can't see the permit in that person's pocket, or the supervisor who sent them there to fix a jammed pallet.

Our how-it-works page uses exactly that kind of example: a restricted zone entry, flagged and sent to a reviewer, who notes "Authorised contractor. Permit on file." and closes it with no action. Nothing happened to anyone, because a person checked before anyone acted. As that page puts it, "a detection is only ever a reason to look closer".

Why the law expects a person in the loop

The Protection of Personal Information Act has a rule on automated decision making. Subject to exceptions set out in the same section, it says a person "may not be subject to a decision which results in legal consequences" for them, or which affects them "to a substantial degree", which is "based solely on the basis of the automated processing of personal information intended to provide a profile of such person including his or her performance at work" or their conduct.

The section then sets out its exceptions, such as certain decisions taken in connection with a contract, or decisions governed by a law or code of conduct that specifies appropriate measures to protect the person's legitimate interests.

The same Act says that when personal information is collected, the responsible party "must take reasonably practicable steps to ensure that the data subject is aware of", among other things, the information being collected and the purpose it's collected for.

Then there's discipline itself. The Code of Good Practice: Dismissal, published in the Government Gazette on 4 September 2025, says "the purpose of a fair procedure is to ensure a genuine dialogue and an opportunity for reflection before any decision is taken", and that a fair procedure is one in which an employee "has been given an adequate and reasonable opportunity to respond to the allegation of misconduct".

Software can raise a flag. It can't hold that dialogue. A person has to.

This is general information about South African law, not legal advice. Your own policies, disciplinary code and any collective agreement matter, so take advice on your own workplace.

How SIG Eagle Eye uses video analytics

SIG Eagle Eye is AI-powered behavioural analytics, classified in line with South African labour law and reviewed by a person. Here's how it works, step by step:

  1. Detect. Configured for your zones and risks, the system picks up behaviour worth a second look: PPE, restricted areas, idle stations, handling of stock and assets. It never looks at toilets, change rooms or any space outside the agreed zones.
  2. Classify. Each event is classified against labour law and your own disciplinary code, so it lands in the right category from the start.
  3. Review. A reviewer confirms the flag, adds context or dismisses it. The system never decides on its own, and nothing is actioned automatically.
  4. Report. A confirmed incident becomes a record of what happened, where, when, which rule applies, who reviewed it and who has accessed it.
  5. Act. Management decides what happens next, following a fair procedure.

A flag isn't a disciplinary record. Only an issue that a person has reviewed and confirmed goes onto a record, and even then the first step is usually a conversation. When the system gets something wrong, the reviewer dismisses the false alarm before it reaches anyone, and the detection is tuned so the same mistake doesn't keep happening.

Employees are told about it, too. Transparent employee notification is a built-in safeguard: the system doesn't operate secretly. The site has a page written for employees that you can share with your team.

Do you need new cameras?

Not necessarily. A SIG Eagle Eye site assessment starts with the coverage you already have and tells you honestly where it falls short. Software can only read what the camera shows it, so that's the right place to start: a camera that can't see the loading bay clearly won't help anyone, with or without software.
