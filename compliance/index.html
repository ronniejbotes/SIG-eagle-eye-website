The law, act by act.

What each law expects of an employer who monitors, disciplines and keeps people safe, and exactly where Eagle Eye does the work.

What the law saysLabour Relations Act 66 of 1995 & the Code of Good Practice: Dismissal

  • Every employee has the right not to be unfairly dismissed (section 185). A dismissal must be for a fair reason and follow a fair procedure (section 188).
  • The 2025 Code of Good Practice: Dismissal replaced Schedule 8 on 4 September 2025. For misconduct it asks whether a rule was broken, whether it was valid and reasonable, whether the employee knew or should have known it, whether it has been applied consistently, and whether dismissal is an appropriate sanction.
  • It favours corrective, progressive discipline, and a procedure that lets the employee respond before any decision is taken.
  • Compensation for an unfair dismissal can reach 12 months' remuneration, or 24 months where the dismissal is automatically unfair (section 194).

Where Eagle Eye does the workEvidence in the shape the Code expects

  • Links every incident to the rule it relates to, in your code or site rules
  • Shows consistency: the same rule, measured the same way, for everyone
  • Separates misconduct from poor performance, because the Code treats them differently
  • Keeps the records of misconduct, action taken and reasons that the Code says employers should maintain

What the law saysBasic Conditions of Employment Act 75 of 1997

  • Ordinary hours are capped at 45 a week (section 9), and overtime only by agreement and within limits (section 10).
  • A meal interval of one hour after five hours of continuous work, which can be reduced to 30 minutes by written agreement (section 14).
  • A daily rest period of 12 consecutive hours and a weekly rest period of 36 (section 15).
  • Night work, after 18:00 and before 06:00, needs agreement, an allowance or reduced hours, and transport at the start and end of the shift (section 17).

Where Eagle Eye does the workTime measured fairly, both ways

  • Time and attendance patterns measured the same way for everyone
  • Shows when the problem is the roster, not the person: missed breaks, long runs of night shifts
  • Facts for an early conversation about time-keeping, long before it becomes a dispute

What the law saysOccupational Health and Safety Act 85 of 1993 & Mine Health and Safety Act 29 of 1996

  • Employers must provide and maintain, as far as reasonably practicable, a working environment that is safe and without risk to employees' health (OHSA section 8).
  • Employees must take reasonable care of their own and others' health and safety, follow health and safety rules and report unsafe situations (OHSA section 14).
  • On mines the MHSA applies, including every employee's right to leave a working place that appears to pose a serious danger (MHSA section 23).

Where Eagle Eye does the workWarnings while there is still time

  • PPE, restricted zones and unsafe acts flagged before they become injuries
  • Consistent safety records that show your rules are monitored and enforced
  • Never treats a worker who leaves danger as a disciplinary matter

What the law saysProtection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013

  • Personal information must be processed lawfully and reasonably (section 9), for a specific, explicitly defined and lawful purpose (section 13).
  • Only what is adequate, relevant and not excessive (section 10), kept no longer than necessary (section 14), and protected against loss and unauthorised access (section 19).
  • People must be told what is collected and why (section 18), may see it and ask for corrections (sections 23 and 24), and security compromises must be reported (section 22).
  • No decision with legal consequences may be based solely on automated processing that profiles a person's performance at work or conduct (section 71).

Where Eagle Eye does the workPrivacy by design, not by promise

  • A purpose defined at setup: safety, conduct, performance and asset protection
  • Only agreed zones and rules; private spaces excluded
  • Controlled, logged access and a retention period set in your policy
  • A person reviews every flag, so software never decides

What the law saysThe Constitution & the Employment Equity Act 55 of 1998

  • Everyone has the right to privacy (section 14) and to fair labour practices (section 23). Rights can be limited only where the limit is reasonable and justifiable (section 36).
  • No person may unfairly discriminate against an employee in any employment policy or practice (EEA section 6).

Where Eagle Eye does the workProportionate and equal

  • Monitoring limited to work areas and a legitimate purpose: proportionate, not total
  • Objective, consistent records that help show discipline is applied equally to everyone

What the law saysEU General Data Protection Regulation

  • For groups with European parents or operations, the GDPR sets a similar standard to POPIA: a lawful basis, purpose limitation, data minimisation and security.
  • It also gives people the right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing that significantly affect them (Article 22).

Where Eagle Eye does the workOne standard across the group

  • The safeguards that serve POPIA are structured in alignment with GDPR principles
  • Simpler group-wide policies for multinational employers

At arbitration

What a commissioner asks. What your record answers.

The questions come straight from the Dismissal Code. Most dismissals that fail at the CCMA fail on one of them. Here is the evidence Eagle Eye puts in front of you for each.

Misconduct · Fairness checklist

  1. Was a rule or standard broken?

    The incident itself: what was observed, where and when, with the clip that matters.

  2. Was the rule valid and reasonable?

    The rule reference from your code or site rules, agreed and brought up to date at setup.

  3. Did the employee know the rule, or should they have?

    The monitoring policy, the rules people signed for, and the signs on the wall.

  4. Has it been applied consistently?

    The same rule, measured the same way for everyone, across shifts and sites.

  5. Is dismissal the appropriate sanction?

    The whole history: counselling, warnings and context, so the sanction can be proportionate.

  6. Was the procedure fair?

    A clear, reviewed record that can be shared in advance, so the employee can prepare and respond.

Video evidence

Will footage stand up at the CCMA?

Arbitrators have accepted video evidence when it is clear, authentic, unaltered and lawfully obtained, and rejected it when it was not. Under section 15 of the ECT Act, a recording can't be refused just because it is electronic. Its weight depends on how reliably it was made and kept.

Employers still lose with cameras on site: footage nobody reviewed ([2023] ZALAC 8), a clip showing only part of the act ([2024] ZALCPE 36), and suspicion offered as proof ([2026] ZALCJHB 50).

A reviewed clip, kept with its record and an access log, is built for exactly that test. Nobody can promise an outcome, but you can go in with evidence that holds its shape.

See Labour Guide on video evidence in arbitration; Electronic Communications and Transactions Act 25 of 2002, section 15; Code of Good Practice: Dismissal, 2025.

If a dismissal goes wrong

What a lost case really costs.

Once the dismissal is established, the employer has to prove it was fair (LRA section 192). If it can't, this is the clock it runs on.

  1. Day 0DismissalThe burden of proof sits with the employer from here.
  2. 30 daysReferralThe time the employee has to take the dispute to the CCMA.
  3. 26 daysConciliationThe CCMA's average turnaround in 2024/25.
  4. 109 daysArbitrationThe average turnaround in 2024/25.
  5. YearsLabour CourtIf it goes to court, it can take years. Nine mine workers dismissed in October 2019 were only ordered back in July 2026.

ReinstatementIt can be backdated to the day of dismissal, with back pay (section 193). The longer the dispute runs, the bigger that bill.

CompensationUp to 12 months' pay, or 24 months where the dismissal was automatically unfair (section 194).

SettlementThe CCMA's settlement rate was 77% in 2024/25. Only 7.8% of cases settled at conciliation put the employee back to work.

POPIA, condition by condition

Eight conditions. Eight answers.

POPIA sets eight conditions for processing personal information lawfully. This is how each one is met when Eagle Eye is on your site.

  1. 1

    Accountability s8

    Roles are written down at setup: who is the responsible party, who reviews, and who may open a record.

  2. 2

    Processing limitation s9 to s12

    Only the zones and rules you agreed, on a lawful basis. Adequate, relevant, not excessive.

  3. 3

    Purpose specification s13 to s14

    A defined purpose, and records deleted once they are no longer needed for it.

  4. 4

    Further processing limitation s15

    Information collected for safety and conduct is not quietly reused for something else.

  5. 5

    Information quality s16

    A person checks every flag, so wrong readings are corrected before they reach a record.

  6. 6

    Openness s17 to s18

    Employees are told what is monitored, why, and who to ask. Signs go up where cameras operate.

  7. 7

    Security safeguards s19 to s22

    Controlled access with a log of every opening, and a plan for reporting any compromise.

  8. 8

    Data subject participation s23 to s25

    Employees can ask what is held about them and ask for it to be corrected.

Legal questions

What employers ask their lawyers

All questions
Is it legal to monitor employees with cameras in South Africa?

Generally yes, in work areas, when employees know about it, the purpose is legitimate and the information is handled in line with POPIA. Private spaces such as toilets and change rooms are off limits. This is general information, so take advice on your own policies.

Do we need employees' consent to monitor them?

Consent is one lawful basis under POPIA, but not the only one. Section 11 also allows processing that is necessary for the employment contract, to comply with a legal obligation such as health and safety, or for your legitimate interests. Whatever the basis, employees must be told what is collected and why.

Will CCTV footage be accepted at the CCMA?

Arbitrators have accepted video evidence when it is clear, shown to be authentic and unaltered, and lawfully obtained, and rejected it when it was not. A reviewed clip kept with its record and an access log is built for that test, although no one can promise an outcome.

Does POPIA allow AI to make decisions about employees?

Not on its own. Section 71 of POPIA says a person may not be subject to a decision with legal consequences, or that affects them substantially, based solely on automated processing that profiles things like their performance at work or conduct. That is why a person reviews every flag and management makes every decision.

Do we have to involve our union?

Collective agreements take precedence over the Dismissal Code, so check yours first. Where you have a recognised union, consult it before monitoring goes live. It is also the fastest way to build trust on the floor.

Who is responsible under POPIA, the employer or SIG Eagle Eye?

Generally the employer is the responsible party for its employees' information, and SIG Eagle Eye processes it on the employer's behalf under a written agreement, as POPIA's rules for operators in sections 20 and 21 require.
