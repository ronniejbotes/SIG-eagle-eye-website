Progress, not the finish line

A record low is still not zero.

South African mines recorded 41 fatalities in 2025, the lowest on record and down from 484 in 1994. Then 2026 turned the wrong way: by 14 September, 49 people had died, already more than in all of 2025.

The industry got here by paying attention to leading indicators: near misses, unsafe acts, the small breaches that come before the serious ones. That is exactly the kind of signal Eagle Eye is built to collect.

2025: provisional Minerals Council South Africa figures, as reported by Daily Maverick, February 2026. 2026: figures given at the Mine Health and Safety Tripartite Summit, as reported by the Sunday Times, September 2026.

Configured for your pit, plant and yard

Five risks, bench by bench.

What Eagle Eye is typically set up to catch on mines, quarries and earthmoving sites.

  1. 01

    Vehicles and pedestrians

    People crossing haul roads outside marked crossings, walking inside a moving machine's zone, light vehicles too close to heavy ones.

  2. 02

    PPE, isolation & exclusion zones

    Hard hats, reflective wear and respirators where they're required. People inside areas that are isolated, barricaded or blasting-restricted.

  3. 03

    Plant & equipment misuse

    Machines started by unauthorised operators, yellow metal moved after hours, equipment idling for long stretches between tasks.

  4. 04

    Diesel, stores & scrap

    Fuel drawn outside refuelling windows, stores entered without authorisation, cable and scrap metal leaving the site. Losses that fund the wrong people.

  5. 05

    Muster, handover & attendance

    Late arrivals at muster, early departures before handover, and teams that aren't where the shift plan says they are.

In the courts

Proof held one case. Lost footage sank another.

Two recent Labour Court cases from South African mines. In one, footage and a consistently enforced rule carried the dismissal. In the other, the footage was stolen, and nine workers were reinstated nearly seven years after they were dismissed.

  1. Labour Court · 2026

    EmployerLost

    The stolen hard drives.

    What happened
    Nine contractor employees at a gold mine's tailings plant were dismissed in October 2019 for an illegal picket during their lunch break. They said they had been rehearsing songs for a colleague's wedding.
    What decided it
    The incident was on camera, but the hard drives were stolen in a hijacking and never recovered, and there were no photos or independent witnesses. In July 2026 the Labour Court found the dismissals unfair and ordered all nine reinstated with back pay.
    What a reviewed record adds
    Reviewed clips stored off site with their record, so one stolen vehicle can't take the evidence with it.
    Judgment: [2026] ZALCJHB 215

  2. Labour Court · 2024

    EmployerWon

    The bag search.

    What happened
    Manganese is valuable, so the mine searched every employee's bag at the exit. A support worker left without having his backpack searched.
    What decided it
    CCTV showed him leaving with a heavy bag. The rule was valid, reasonable and consistently enforced, and the footage established the breach. The dismissal was upheld.
    What a reviewed record adds
    Proof that the rule is applied at every exit, on every shift, to everyone: the consistency that made this case winnable.
    Judgment: [2024] ZALCJHB 139

The lessonFootage only protects you if it's reviewed, stored safely and backed up. A stolen hard drive shouldn't take your whole case with it.

The law on a mine

On a mine, the MHSA sets the rules.

The Mine Health and Safety Act 29 of 1996 places duties on the mine and on every employee. Eagle Eye classifications are configured around both, and around your own codes of practice and standards.

Employees must, under section 22

  • Take reasonable care of their own health and safety, and that of others
  • Use and look after the protective equipment provided
  • Report risks they can't deal with to their supervisor
  • Comply with prescribed health and safety measures

Section 23 · The right to walk away

Leaving danger is never misconduct.

Every employee may leave a working place that, with reasonable justification, appears to pose a serious danger to their health or safety. Eagle Eye records that withdrawal as exactly what it is. It is never classified as absence without leave or refusal to work.

Mine Health and Safety Act 29 of 1996, sections 22 and 23. Read the Act.

Earthmoving and civil contractors: on construction sites, the Occupational Health and Safety Act and its Construction Regulations apply instead of the MHSA. Eagle Eye is configured to whichever regime governs the site you're on.

Mining questions

All questions
Which safety law applies on a mine, OHSA or the MHSA?

On mines, the Mine Health and Safety Act 29 of 1996 sets the health and safety duties. On construction and civil earthmoving sites, the Occupational Health and Safety Act and its Construction Regulations apply. Eagle Eye is configured to the regime that governs your site.

Does it work underground?

Surface operations, workshops, stores, fuel points, plant yards and access points are the usual starting point. Underground coverage depends on your infrastructure and is assessed per site.

Does it replace our safety officers or health and safety representatives?

No. It gives them a consistent record of what happened. People still review every incident and make every decision.

What if someone leaves a working place because it's dangerous?

Section 23 of the MHSA protects that right. Eagle Eye is configured so that withdrawing from danger is recorded as exactly that, and is never classified as absence or misconduct.
